<objective>
Put the settled europium-nitrogen binding curve in front of a human and let them judge it. This is
the phase's final gate.

**Plain-language orientation.** A binding curve is the molecule's energy plotted against how far
apart its two atoms are. It should fall as the atoms approach and find each other, reach a lowest
point where the bond is most comfortable, then rise steeply as they are pushed too close. A single
clean well. Anything else - two wells, a straight slope, a flat line - says something is wrong.

The user set this as the test, in their own words: *"The final test should be me looking at the newly
generated SCF graph and telling you whether it looks good or not."* They have done exactly this once
before, for the one-pass curve in Phase 4, and approved it.

Purpose: decision D-6.02 makes human judgement of a freshly computed graph the phase's completion
bar, in place of a numeric one. Decision D-6.09 requires both curves overlaid with the separations
where the loop gave up visibly marked, because the human cannot judge "does this look right" without
the approved curve beside it, and cannot interpret a wild point without knowing the solver gave up
there.

Output: a script that recomputes everything from live code, a figure, and a recorded human verdict.
</objective>

## Design decision - what the figure shows

Decision D-6.09 mandates two curves overlaid with failures marked. This plan adds one more series and
one more panel, both for a reason:

**Panel 1, energy against separation**, three series on one axis:

1. the one-pass curve - the reference the human already approved in Phase 4;
2. the settled curve at per-atom charge resolution - the D-6.09 comparison;
3. the settled curve at per-orbital-group charge resolution - what plans 06-02 and 06-03 built.

The third series is there because it is this phase's other deliverable, and a human whose gate is
"does it look right" should be shown the thing rather than told about it. It is labelled distinctly
so it cannot be confused with the mandated pair.

**Panel 2, charge moved onto nitrogen against separation**, same three series. This is where a
runaway is legible. The failing runs moved charge to about 3 or about 5 electrons where a healthy
answer sits near 0.6; on the energy axis those points are off-scale and squash the well flat, but on
the charge axis they are unmistakable. Phase 4's sign-off already found that a single linear energy
axis could not support the shape judgement being asked for, and was resolved with a second panel;
this repeats that lesson rather than rediscovering it.

**Failures are marked on both panels** with a distinct marker on the failing point plus a count in
the legend, so "converged at 19 of 21 separations" is readable without counting markers.

Reversibility: **easy**. One script and one image.

  <what-built>
    Everything this phase produced, presented as one picture.

    **What was wrong.** The repeat-until-settled charge loop ran away at 9 of 21 atom separations for
    the europium-nitrogen molecule. Electrons piled entirely onto one atom and the energy came back at
    +198 eV, against a true scale near -17 eV. For scale, a typical chemical bond is worth 2-5 eV, so
    +198 eV is not an error, it is nonsense.

    **What caused it.** Not the f orbitals. A convergence-speedup routine called the Krylov
    accelerator, which switches on after the tenth pass. With it off, all 21 separations settle, and at
    the 12 separations where it did work the two runs agree to better than one millionth of an eV - so
    it was never finding a different answer, it was failing to find the one the plain method finds
    reliably. The same thing showed up in 16 unrelated two-atom molecules. Repairing it was deferred
    to a later phase by your ruling; this phase switches it off for f-containing molecules only, so no
    existing calculation on any other element changes by a digit.

    **What was built.**

    1. The loop now **reports** what happened - the number of passes it took, or `-1` meaning it gave
       up - instead of only printing a line nobody can act on. All four charge loops in the codebase
       report the same way.
    2. The electron-repulsion matrix now covers f orbitals. Seven combinations were missing, and six
       more were quietly skipping every f-containing pair, so the matrix came back with whole rows of
       zeros - finite, correctly shaped, and wrong. All sixteen combinations are now filled.
    3. The charge loop can now track charge **one orbital group at a time** rather than one lump per
       atom. This matters concretely: the old code charged europium the repulsion strength of its s
       group, 5.71 eV, while seven of its nine outer electrons live in its f group, whose real
       strength is 13.61 eV - 2.4 times more. That is the third curve on the plot.
    4. A written verdict on the 10.6 eV gap between the one-pass and settled energies, which concludes
       it is a difference of **definition**, not a defect: the one-pass path carries no
       electron-repulsion term at all, by design, so the two numbers answer different questions and
       must never be compared as if they were the same measurement.

    **What was deliberately not done.** No number produced by this phase was written into a test as a
    reference value - your instruction was "do not freeze these numbers in yet, we don't know if
    there's a bug". Phase 4's one-pass reference energy is untouched and still green. And no test
    requires the settled curve's lowest point to land inside Phase 4's tolerance band; you were offered
    that bar and did not take it.
  </what-built>

## Trust Boundaries

A local scientific library: no network, no authentication, no untrusted input, no user data. The
boundary that matters in this plan is between **what the figure shows and what a human concludes from
it**. Every threat below is a way for a picture to mislead an honest reader.

| Boundary | Description |
|---|---|
| computed numbers -> figure | A point where the loop gave up is a number like any other. Plotted without a mark, it reads as a physical feature. |
| figure -> human verdict | The human's judgement is the phase's completion bar. A figure drawn from stale data would produce a sign-off on a tree that no longer exists. |
| verdict -> phase state | A recorded verdict decides whether the phase completes. A negative verdict that is logged and stepped over converts a gate into a note. |
